Why go

Why Visit Carcassonne?

Carcassonne is one of Europe's largest surviving walled cities, with roots that run from a Gallo-Roman hilltop through the Cathar wars to a royal frontier fortress. It was nearly torn down in the 1800s — and instead became a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Walking the Cité is walking through almost two thousand years of siege, faith and survival.

Two concentric rings of walls, some three kilometres long, studded with 52 towers, wrap the medieval streets. Inside stand the Château Comtal and the Basilica of Saints Nazarius and Celsus, with its famous stained glass. The pointed slate roofs you see today came from the 19th-century restoration by Viollet-le-Duc.

The city's name is tied to the legend of Dame Carcas, who is said to have outlasted a siege by a clever trick — and rang the bells in triumph, "Carcas sonne." The medieval tile game that sold millions carries the city's name too. Folklore and fame, both carved into the gates.

Between the inner and outer walls runs the wide corridor of the lices — the lists — where you can walk the full circuit of the defences. It is the best way to see how the fortress actually worked, and how impossible it must have looked to any army standing below.

The story

The Story of Carcassonne

Dame Carcas and the Bells That Named the City

The best-known legend explains the city's very name. During a long siege — tradition places it under Charlemagne — Carcassonne was near starvation. A woman named Dame Carcas took command, propped straw dummies on the walls to fake a full garrison, and, with the last of the grain, fattened the last pig and threw it over the ramparts. The besiegers saw food being thrown away, assumed the city could hold out forever, and left. As they went, the bells rang and a herald cried "Carcas sonne!" — "Carcas rings." A carved bust of Dame Carcas still greets you at the Narbonne Gate.

From Roman Hilltop to Visigothic Stronghold

Long before the medieval towers, this hill was a fortified Gallo-Roman town. The inner ring of walls still holds Roman-era stonework, recognisable by its narrow courses and rounded towers. After Rome, the Visigoths held and strengthened the site — and, as local legend has it, hid a fabled treasure here. Roman below, medieval above: that layering is why the walls feel so deep in time.

The Cathars and the Siege of 1209

In the early 13th century Carcassonne was a seat of the Trencavel viscounts, swept up in the Albigensian Crusade against the Cathars. In 1209 the young viscount Raymond-Roger Trencavel defended the city — but with the wells failing and the town swollen with refugees, it fell in a fortnight. Carcassonne passed to the crusaders and then the French crown, ending local independence and beginning its life as a royal fortress.

The Royal Fortress of Saint Louis

Under the French kings, and especially Louis IX (Saint Louis), Carcassonne was rebuilt as a cutting-edge border fortress. A second, outer ring of walls rose around the older one, creating the double defences that make the city so unmistakable today. A new lower town, the Bastide Saint-Louis, was laid out across the Aude. For four centuries the Cité guarded the frontier with the Crown of Aragon.

Decay, Demolition — and Viollet-le-Duc

When the border with Spain moved south in 1659, Carcassonne lost its purpose and slid into ruin. By the 1800s the state proposed demolishing the walls. Locals fought to save it, and from 1853 the architect Eugène Viollet-le-Duc began a sweeping restoration. His pointed slate roofs — which critics say belong further north — gave the Cité the fairy-tale silhouette now recognised worldwide, and even helped inspire the world's best-selling medieval tile game.

Good to know

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Carcassonne free to visit?

Yes — the medieval Cité, its streets and squares are free to walk. The Château Comtal and the wall-walk along the ramparts require a separate paid ticket.

How long do you need at Carcassonne?

Plan at least half a day for the Cité — the ramparts, the castle and the basilica. A full day lets you add the lower town, the Old Bridge and a relaxed meal.

Is the audio tour available offline?

Yes. Download it once in the TouringBee app and it works fully offline, with no data needed inside the walls. The offline map shows where you are; you start each track yourself at the spot.

What languages is the audio guide in?

Eight: English, French, German, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Polish and Russian.

Does it work on iPhone and Android?

Yes, the TouringBee app runs on both iOS and Android.

How do I get to Carcassonne?

By train it is about 50 minutes from Toulouse and easy from Narbonne; the Cité is a roughly 15-minute uphill walk from the station and lower town, or a short taxi. Carcassonne also has its own small airport.

Why is Carcassonne so famous?

It is one of the largest and best-preserved walled cities in Europe, with a double ring of ramparts and 52 towers. Nearly demolished in the 1800s, it was restored by Viollet-le-Duc and is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Who was Dame Carcas?

According to legend she defended the city during a siege and tricked the attackers into leaving, then rang the bells in victory — "Carcas sonne." It is folklore rather than proven history, but it gave the city its name and its emblem.
