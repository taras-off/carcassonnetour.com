What to See in Carcassonne

One headline sight, a single ticket worth buying, and the free extras most day-trippers walk past — in the order that makes sense.

By Eugene · Updated September 2026

Most “what to see in Carcassonne” lists get one thing wrong: they hand you twenty bullet points as if the town were a checklist. Carcassonne really does have one headline sight — the Cité. But it isn't a single monument; it's a whole walled medieval town, which is why most of the city's main sights are either inside it or a short walk across the river.

And the most practical thing first: entering the Cité itself is free. What you pay for is mainly the Château Comtal and access to part of the ramparts. So what follows isn't a list of twenty things — it's the handful that actually earns your time, in an order that makes sense, with a clear note on what's free and what needs a ticket.

One more thing. Every one of these stones has a story — who Dame Carcas was and why the city carries her name, why the fortress has two rings of walls instead of one, what happened here during the crusade against the Cathars. I'm not going to spoil those here; they come alive best where they happened. And if you want not just to see the walls but to understand why they were built and what happened here, there's a TouringBee audio walk through the Cité: about two hours, a ready-made route, with map and audio fully offline.

The Cité — the double walls and 52 towers (free)

Start with the obvious, because the obvious is the whole point. The Cité is a UNESCO-listed medieval town wrapped in two concentric rings of ramparts — roughly three kilometres of walls and fifty-two towers. The streets, the squares, both great gate-towers, the views over the lower town and the accessible stretches of the space between the two wall lines are all free to see. Come early or in the evening and it's quiet and golden; come at midday in summer and it's hot and packed. If you see only one thing in Carcassonne, it's this, and it doesn't cost a cent.

The Porte Narbonnaise — the main gate (free)

The eastern gate is how most people enter, and it's worth slowing down for rather than walking straight through. Two tall towers, a drawbridge line, the narrowing approach — it's the most photographed way into the Cité for a reason. If you come from the city's car parks by the fortress or from the tour coaches, this is most likely where you'll enter. Free, always open, and the natural starting point for a walk.

The Château Comtal and the ramparts wall-walk (ticket)

Inside the free city sits the one main paid sight: the Château Comtal, the castle-within-the-castle, and the wall-top rampart walk that comes with it on the same ticket. It's the ticket that gets you up onto the upper stretches of the ramparts and the views you can't get from the free route. Go late morning, before the peak, and in summer book a timed slot so you walk straight in. If you're short on time or budget you can skip it — the Cité alone gives you a few hours' worth — but if you buy one ticket here, this is the one.

The Basilica of Saints Nazarius and Celsus (free)

The Cité's church is easy to walk past and a mistake to miss. From outside it's plain; inside it holds its celebrated stained glass, glowing along the choir. Entry is free. Two practical notes: it keeps seasonal hours (roughly 9:00–19:00 in the warmer months, 10:00–17:30 in winter), and on Sunday mornings, from about 10:45 to 12:30, Mass is held and visits aren't possible then — come outside that window if you want the interior to yourself.

The space between the walls — les lices (free)

Here's the part that's easy to miss if you only follow the main tourist street. The French call this wide grassy corridor between the inner and outer walls les lices — it's the space that made the double defence work. You can walk accessible stretches of it for free, with the towers rising on both sides and almost none of the crowd from the main street. It's the best free way to feel the scale of the place.

The Pont Vieux and the Bastide Saint-Louis (free)

Cross the river on the Pont Vieux — the 14th-century pedestrian bridge — and two things happen. First, you get the postcard: the classic panorama of the whole Cité — golden by day, floodlit by night — is from the bridge and the far bank, and it's free. Second, you reach the lower town, the Bastide Saint-Louis: a calm grid of streets, squares and cafés that most day-trippers skip. Its heart is Place Carnot, with an open-air market on Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday mornings. This is where to eat and to wait out the midday heat.

The Canal du Midi (free)

If you arrive or leave by train, you're two minutes from the Canal du Midi, the UNESCO-listed 17th-century waterway lined with plane trees. Flat, green and shaded — a quiet walk that has nothing to do with battlements, and a good way to end the day.

FAQ

What to see in Carcassonne — questions

What is the number-one thing to see in Carcassonne?

The Cité — the walled medieval town itself. It's a whole fortified city, not a single monument, and walking its streets, gates and ramparts is free.

Is there an entry fee for Carcassonne?

No fee to enter the medieval city or to walk its streets, squares and the accessible stretches between the walls, and the basilica is free too. Of the main sights on this list, a ticket is needed for the Château Comtal and the paid rampart walk (€13 Oct–Mar, €19 Apr–Sep; under 18 free for everyone, 18–25 free for EU nationals).

Is the Basilica of Saints Nazarius and Celsus worth seeing?

Yes, and it's free. The stained glass is one of its main draws. Just avoid the Sunday-morning Mass window (about 10:45–12:30) if you want to see the interior.

What do most visitors miss in Carcassonne?

Three free things: les lices (the grassy corridor between the two walls), the panorama of the Cité from the Pont Vieux and the far riverbank, and the lower town, the Bastide Saint-Louis, with its market on Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday.

How long do you need to see Carcassonne?

Half a day covers the Cité and the castle; a full day lets you add the basilica, les lices, the bridge and the lower town. See our one-day plan for the order that avoids the heat and the crowds.

Do you need a guide to visit Carcassonne?

Not to walk in — the city is free and open. But it's easy to see beautiful walls without understanding why there are two rings of them or why Carcassonne looks the way it does. A self-guided audio tour is the easy way to get the history where it happened, without a group or a schedule.
